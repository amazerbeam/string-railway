# Channel playbooks

**Scope.** What is here is *structural* — the shape of a video, the anatomy of a store page, how
to behave in a community. Those hold across years. What is **not** here, deliberately: current
reach figures, wishlist thresholds, algorithm behaviour, festival dates and rules. Those move
every year and are resolved live (SKILL.md → "Resolve live sources"). If you find yourself wanting
to write a number into this file, it belongs in `content-plan.md` with a checked-on date instead.

---

## YouTube — long devlog

The home base. This is where someone decides they like the developer, not just the game.

**Shape of an 8–20 minute devlog:**

1. **Cold open, under 20 seconds** — the problem or the result, shown. Not a greeting, not a
   channel intro, not "in today's video".
2. **The stakes** — why this mattered, in one or two sentences. A viewer who does not know why
   they should care leaves here.
3. **The work** — the actual middle. Show the broken version before the fixed one. Failed
   attempts are content, not embarrassment; cutting them removes the reason to watch.
4. **The turn** — what was learned, or what changed the developer's mind.
5. **Where it stands** — honest current state, and one sentence on what's next.

**Practices that carry:**

- Talking head plus screen capture, cut tight. Silence and dead air are the enemy, not roughness.
- Title states the specific thing, not the abstract one. "I rebuilt my card game's whole scoring
  system" beats "Devlog #4".
- Thumbnail: one readable idea at phone size. Two words maximum if any.
- Consistency of subject matters more than consistency of schedule — a channel about one game
  being made teaches the algorithm who to show it to.
- Pin a comment with the Steam link. Put it in the description's first line too.

## YouTube Shorts and other short-form

Discovery. The job is one idea, delivered before the viewer's thumb moves.

- **Hook in the first second** — the thing itself, mid-motion. No wind-up.
- One idea per clip. A clip explaining two mechanics explains neither.
- 15–35 seconds is the working band for a mechanic clip; longer only if the payoff genuinely
  builds.
- Text on screen, legible without sound, because most viewing is silent.
- Loop the ending back to the start where the clip allows it.
- **For a deckbuilder specifically:** the number going up is the format. A run compounding into an
  absurd single-trick score, a build that shouldn't work working, a near-death reversal — these
  are self-contained clips that need no explanation and no context.
- Repost across feeds. The same clip on a second platform is not a wasted clip.

**Recurring clip formats worth keeping in the backlog:**

| Format | The hook |
|---|---|
| The absurd number | A run compounding past all reason |
| The bug | Something visually wrong, played straight |
| Before / after | Old UI or old rule against the new one, cut hard |
| "This card is a mistake" | A card that is obviously overtuned, demonstrated |
| The rebuild | Prototype footage beside the Unity version |
| The near-loss | One health left, a reversal |

## Steam page

The destination. Everything else exists to get someone here.

**Anatomy, in the order a visitor consumes it:**

1. **Capsule art** — seen at thumbnail size in a grid of hundreds. Readable small, or invisible.
2. **Short description** — the genre and the twist, in one sentence a stranger can repeat to a
   friend. This is the hardest sentence in the project; draft it many times.
3. **Trailer** — gameplay in the first five seconds. No logo card first. Show the loop, show the
   thing that is different, end on the wishlist prompt.
4. **Screenshots** — five to eight, each showing a different part of the loop, all in-game.
5. **Long description** — skimmable. What you do, what makes a run different from the last one,
   what the player is choosing between.
6. **Tags** — genre-honest. Wrong tags put the page in front of people who bounce.

Put the page up as soon as there is honest footage. It accrues wishlists the entire time it is
live, and it cannot start accruing before it exists.

## Reddit

High value, high risk. The only approach that works is being a member of the community before
being a poster to it.

- Participate for weeks before posting anything of your own.
- Genre-specific subreddits convert better than large general gaming ones — the audience is
  pre-qualified.
- Screenshot Saturday is the reliable recurring slot.
- Read each subreddit's self-promotion rule before posting; they differ and they are enforced.
- Post the interesting thing, not the announcement. "Here's how I made skulls invert a trick"
  survives; "wishlist my game" does not.
- Reply to every comment. The thread's life depends on it.

## Discord

Small, loyal, slow. The people here wishlist, playtest, and buy on day one.

- Do not open a server before there is a reason for people to be in it — an empty server reads as
  abandoned and is hard to recover from.
- The value exchange is access: early builds, votes on decisions, seeing the mess first.
- One channel for updates, one for feedback, one for off-topic. More channels than members is a
  failure state.
- Playtest recruitment happens here first, before anywhere public.

## Trailer beat sheet (keep in `steam-page.md`)

For when a trailer gets drafted, the beats in order:

1. Gameplay, immediately — the most legible five seconds of the loop.
2. The hook: the one thing this game does that the neighbouring games do not.
3. Escalation: three or four cuts showing variety of run, build, or outcome.
4. The best moment in the game, held slightly longer than comfortable.
5. Title card, then wishlist prompt. In that order, at the end, briefly.

No voiceover unless the developer wants to be in it. No text explaining rules — if a rule needs
explaining in a trailer, show its consequence instead.
