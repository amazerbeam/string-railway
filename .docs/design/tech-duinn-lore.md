# Tech Duinn — the setting, and the myth behind every name

**What this document is.** The game is set in Tech Duinn, and almost every name in it is drawn from
Irish myth. This file records *which* myth, *why that figure and not another*, and *what was invented
rather than inherited*. It exists for three readers: whoever writes the game's text, whoever draws
its art, and the player — because the last section specifies a screen that shows them all of this
in-game.

**What this document is not.** It is not the rules and it is not a feature list.
`.docs/game_rules/the-hunt.md` owns the rules; `.docs/implementation/feature-inventory-for-the-port.md`
owns what exists and holds the authoritative rename table. This file owns only the fiction and the
sourcing. Where it names a rule, it does so to show why a name was chosen — never as the record of
what that rule is.

---

## 1. The setting

**Tech Duinn — the House of Donn.** In Irish tradition Donn is the first of the Milesians to die on
reaching Ireland, and he becomes the god of the dead. His house is a rock off the south-west coast,
out past the last land, and the dead gather there before passing onward. The line attributed to him
is the whole premise of this game in one sentence: *to me, to my house, you shall all come after
death.*

Three properties of the Irish Otherworld matter for how the game should look and feel, and all three
are inherited rather than invented:

- **It is alongside, not above or below.** You reach it through a mound, through mist, or across
  water — not by ascending or descending. So Tech Duinn is a room you walk into, not a realm you are
  cast down to.
- **Time runs differently there.** Oisín spends what feels like three years in the Otherworld and
  returns to find three hundred have passed. A run can take as long as it takes; nothing outside is
  waiting.
- **It is not a punishment.** Tech Duinn is a gathering place, not a hell. The dead are not being
  tormented — they are waiting, and while they wait they do what Irish people have always done around
  the dead.

**Which is the wake.** An Irish wake is a night spent with a body: drinking, storytelling, and games.
Wake games are a real and well-documented tradition, sometimes rough ones. So the game's central
image — you, seated across a table from a stranger, playing cards by firelight with the dead in the
room — needs no invention at all. It is what a wake *is*.

**What the player is playing for.** Passage. You are newly arrived and you have not yet gone onward.
Everyone at the table is in the same position, and the cards decide who moves.

## 2. The three suits

The suits are three named creatures of Irish myth rather than three categories of object. This is why
they never pluralise — there is no "Nine of Hounds", only **the Nine of the Hound**. Each is a
specific animal from a specific story.

**The Raven.** The Morrígan takes the form of a carrion crow, and lands on Cú Chulainn's shoulder
when he finally dies — which is how his enemies know it is safe to approach. The raven in Irish myth
is not a bad omen so much as a *true* one: it tells you the outcome has already been decided.

**The Salmon.** The Salmon of Knowledge fed on the hazelnuts that fell into the Well of Segais, and
whoever ate it would know everything there is to know. Fionn burns his thumb on it while cooking it
for someone else and gets the knowledge by accident. It is the suit of things known, and of knowledge
arriving through the back door.

**The Hound.** Cú Chulainn's name means "the Hound of Culann" — he kills the smith's guard dog and
then serves as the dog himself until a replacement is reared. The hound is loyalty enforced by
obligation: a bargain you are held to whether you like it or not, which is the right note for a game
where following suit is framed as an oath.

**On the colours.** Raven takes black and blue-black, Salmon takes silver going to red, Hound takes
tan and bone. These read apart at pixel resolution and in greyscale, which is the real constraint.

## 3. The cast — the six named ranks

Every name **leads with a word the player already knows** and then gives the mythological name. The
card face shows only the leading word; the full name appears on hover, in the dossier, and in the
codex described in §6.

### Banshee Clíodhna — the 1

**On the card:** Banshee. **In full:** Banshee Clíodhna.

The *bean sídhe* is the woman of the mounds, and she keens for a death before it happens. She is not
the cause of the death and never has been — she attends it. Clíodhna is named in Munster tradition as
a queen of the banshees, associated with the sea and with the wave that bears her name at Glandore.

**Why this card.** The 1 hands the lead to whichever side *lost* the trick. That is the banshee's
role exactly: she goes to the losing side, and her arrival is what moves things forward.

### Shapeshifter Púca — the 3

**On the card:** Shapeshifter. **In full:** Shapeshifter Púca.

The púca changes shape — horse, goat, hare, man — and its attentions are unpredictable rather than
straightforwardly hostile. It might ruin your harvest or it might save your life, and it is the same
creature either way.

**Why this card.** The 3 lets you name any suit as trump, changing what the whole hand is about
mid-trick. Nothing else in Irish myth is *defined* by changing what it is.

### Blacksmith Goibniu — the 5

**On the card:** Blacksmith. **In full:** Blacksmith Goibniu.

Goibniu is the smith of the Tuatha Dé Danann, one of the three craft-gods, and he forges the weapons
they win with — reputedly in three strokes, and his blades never miss. He also brews the ale at the
Otherworld feast that keeps the gods from ageing.

**Why this card.** The 5 takes a card away and hands back a fresh one, and raises how often you can
do that. Taking a thing and returning a better one is a smith's whole function.

**Why "blacksmith" and not "smith".** Bare "Smith" reads as a surname, the way "Sergeant Smith" does,
and needed a definite article to fix it — "The Smith Goibniu" — which made it the only name in the
cast carrying one. "Blacksmith" is unmistakably a trade, so the article goes and the name matches the
shape of the other five.

### Graverobber Bres — the 7

**On the card:** Graverobber. **In full:** Graverobber Bres.

Bres the Beautiful is half-Fomorian and briefly king of the Tuatha Dé. He is remembered for two
things: being extremely good-looking, and taxing his own people into misery while offering no
hospitality — the specific failing that gets an Irish king deposed. He is thrown out and starts a war
to get the throne back.

**Why this card.** The 7 pays you an escalating bonus on tricks you win and costs you double health
on tricks you lose. It is a bargain that looks generous and turns on you, which is Bres's entire
story.

**Why "graverobber" and not "king".** The epithet has to teach the mechanic, and "king" teaches
nothing. Taking from the dead and paying for it later is the reading the player needs, and
in the house of the dead it lands harder.

### War Goddess Morrígan — the 9

**On the card:** War Goddess. **In full:** War Goddess Morrígan.

The Morrígan decides battles by attending them. She appears to Cú Chulainn before his death, and
when he refuses her she turns against him — attacking as an eel, a wolf, and a red heifer in turn.
She is frequently a *triad*, three figures who are also one.

**Why this card.** A lone 9 counts as trump and beats what would otherwise win. Two 9s cancel each
other out. The cancellation is the hard part to justify in the abstract and needs no justification
here: her aspects are separate figures, and two of her in one place is her at odds with herself.

### Champion Bricriu — the 11

**On the card:** Champion. **In full:** Champion Bricriu.

Bricriu of the Poison Tongue throws a feast and promises the champion's portion — the best cut of
the meat, which by right goes to the greatest warrior present — to three heroes at once. Cú Chulainn,
Conall and Lóegaire all take him at his word, and the rest of the story is them being made to prove
which of them it belongs to. Bricriu wins nothing and fights nobody. His whole art is making other
people show what they are worth.

**Why this card.** The 11, when led, forces the other side to put forward their best card of that
suit. That is the champion's portion exactly: a demand, not a display of strength. And it binds both
sides equally, which usually needs explaining — here it does not, because everyone seated at
Bricriu's feast is under the same obligation, including whoever called for it.

**Why "champion" and not his name on the face.** The leading word has to teach the mechanic, and what
the card demands is a champion — the follower's best. "Poison Tongue" was the other candidate and it
reads as damage, which the card does not do.

**Why not Nuada.** The 11 was First King Nuada until 2026-09-04. It was dropped because "First King"
teaches the player nothing about what the card does, and because Nuada's story is a king *losing* his
throne — the opposite note for a card that compels. Two others were weighed and rejected: the Hag of
Sovereignty, whose story is about accepting a hard thing rather than forcing someone else's hand, and
Balor, whose eye nothing can hide from but who is wanted intact as a late opponent.

## 4. The rest of the vocabulary

**The Shade.** Your opponent at the table — a soul that has arrived and not yet gone onward. Not a
quarry, because nothing here is being hunted; you are two of the same thing, seated opposite.

**The Wake.** One encounter. See §1.

**Charms.** Small carried objects that do one thing under one condition. Irish folk practice is full
of them — a rowan cross over the door, a coin kept for luck, a stone carried in a pocket — and the
important property is that a charm is *spent*: it works once and it is gone. Which is exactly what
these cards do.

**Dagda's Cauldron.** The Dagda's cauldron is one of the Four Treasures of the Tuatha Dé, and its
defining property is that no company ever leaves it unsatisfied. As a source that hands out cards
forever without emptying, the mapping is direct.

**Goibniu's Ale.** The free heal the player carries, refilled by beating a stage boss. Goibniu brews
the ale at the Otherworld feast, and drinking it is what keeps the Tuatha Dé from ageing or sickening
— so a healing draught with his name on it is inherited rather than invented. It also gives the smith
a second appearance: the player meets him on the 5 and then again in the shop.

**The Cairn.** A cairn is a stone pile raised over the dead that every passer-by adds one stone to.
It is the only structure in this fiction that is *built by the accumulated dead* — which is what
cross-run progression is. Each finished run leaves a stone; the pile outlives the run that laid it.

**Oathbreak.** Following the lead suit is presented to the player, in the tutorial, as an oath they
are bound by rather than as a rule of the game. Breaking an oath in Irish myth is never free and
never impossible — it is a thing you may do, knowingly, and then live with. That framing is what
makes the card interesting instead of a cheat code.

**The Omen.** The card turned face up to rule the hand. An omen in this tradition does not cause the
outcome, it discloses it — which is precisely what the trump card does.

**Surge.** The multiplier reward. Renamed off "Momentum" because momentum is physics vocabulary and
its two siblings, Blade and Guard, are not.

## 5. What is deliberately not mythological

Recorded so that a later reader does not "finish the job" and make the game worse.

**The skull stays a skull.** A geas — the binding taboo that makes the obviously right action the
wrong one — is the more authentic name and a closer mechanical fit. It was rejected on purpose. A
skull tells a player to avoid the thing on sight with no teaching at all, and that legibility is worth
more than the accuracy. Everything built on it keeps the plain word too.

**Total, roll, pot and coins stay.** Gambling vocabulary at a wake is correct rather than wrong.

**Hearts and the bronze/silver/gold tiers stay.** They are doing plain mechanical work and dressing
them up would cost clarity and buy nothing.

## 6. The codex — showing the player the meaning

The developer's intent, recorded here as a specification rather than an aspiration: **there should be
a place in the game where a player can read what all of this means.** Most of the research above is
invisible during play, and the fiction is doing real work — a player who knows who Bres was
understands the 7 better than one who does not.

**Shape.** A codex reachable from the menu and from the pre-fight stop, with one entry per name. Each
entry carries the full name, the myth in a short paragraph, and one line on how the card's rule
mirrors it. The last part is the point: the codex is not decoration, it is the game explaining its own
design.

**What has an entry.** The setting itself, the three suits, the six named ranks, and each renamed
term from §4. Roughly twenty entries.

**Unlocking.** Entries should unlock on first encounter rather than being available from the start —
seeing the card, or facing the opponent, opens the entry. This turns the codex into a record of the
run rather than a wall of text at the main menu, and it gives the player a reason to open it at the
moment the name means something to them.

**Where the hover fits.** The card face carries only the leading word. Hovering a card shows the full
name and one line of myth; the codex is where the full entry lives. The hover is the hook, the codex
is the payoff.

## 7. Sourcing — what is inherited and what is invented

Stated plainly so nobody later mistakes an invention for a source, or "corrects" a deliberate choice.

**Inherited, and standard.** Donn and Tech Duinn as the gathering place of the dead. The wake and its
games. The Morrígan as war goddess, as triad, and as the crow on Cú Chulainn's shoulder. Bricriu's
feast and the champion's portion. Goibniu as smith of the Tuatha Dé. Bres as the ungenerous king who was
deposed. The Salmon of Knowledge and the Well of Segais. Cú Chulainn's name and the story behind it.
The banshee as a keener who attends death rather than causing it. The púca as shapeshifter. The
Dagda's cauldron as one of the Four Treasures.

**Inherited, but regional or contested.** Clíodhna as a queen of the banshees — this is Munster
tradition rather than a pan-Irish account, and elsewhere she is a goddess of the sea and of love with
no banshee association at all. It is a legitimate reading, not the only one.

**Invented for this game.** Bres as a *graverobber* — he is a bad king in the sources, not a robber of
graves. The púca choosing what trump is. Goibniu governing an exchange of cards. The banshee handing
anyone the lead. Every one of these takes a real figure and gives them a job the myth does not record,
which is normal practice for a game and worth being honest about.

**Not used, and why.** Cú Chulainn himself has no card and no opponent slot: the Hound is a suit now,
and the collision would be constant.
